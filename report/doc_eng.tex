\subsection{A Survey of Large Language Models for Financial Applications: Progress, Prospects and Challenges \cite{zhao2023survey}}

\subsubsection{Research Focus}
The survey reviews how LLMs are applied in finance, emphasizing their role in:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sentiment analysis
    \item Time-series forecasting
    \item Financial reasoning and decision support
    \item Agent-based modeling for trading and simulation
\end{itemize}
It highlights both the potential and the challenges of adapting LLMs to financial NLP tasks.

\subsubsection{Methodology}
The authors conduct a structured literature review and classify applications into five major categories (see Table~\ref{tab:finance-llm-taxonomy}). They also summarize widely used datasets and benchmarks.

\midrule
Linguistic tasks & Summarization of earnings calls, information extraction from filings \\

Agent-based modeling & LLM-powered trading agents, market simulations \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\label{tab:finance-llm-taxonomy}
\end{table}

\subsubsection{Results}
\begin{itemize}
    \item LLMs outperform traditional models on financial NLP tasks.
    \item Domain-specific pretraining (e.g., FinBERT, FinGPT) further boosts accuracy.
    \item Benchmarks like FPB, FiQA, and FinQA confirm improvements in sentiment, QA, and reasoning.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Data scarcity and domain adaptation difficulties
    \item Risk of look-ahead bias in financial modeling
    \item High computational cost and latency
    \item Lack of interpretability and explainability
    \item Legal, compliance, and regulatory risks
\end{itemize}

\subsection{FinBERT: Financial Sentiment Analysis with Pre-trained Language Models \cite{araci2019finbert}}

\subsubsection{Research Focus}
FinBERT addresses the challenge of sentiment analysis in financial texts, where general sentiment models often fail due to specialized jargon and domain-specific context.  
The goal is to adapt BERT for accurate classification of financial news, reports, and statements.

\subsubsection{Methodology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pre-trains BERT on a large financial corpus (e.g., news, company filings, analyst reports).
    \item Fine-tunes on benchmark financial sentiment datasets for three-class classification (positive, negative, neutral).
    \item Compares performance with traditional ML and generic BERT sentiment models.
\end{itemize}

    \item Demonstrates robustness across multiple datasets (e.g., Financial PhraseBank, FiQA).

\subsection{Deep reinforcement learning for automated stock trading: An ensemble strategy \cite{li2020ensemble}}

\subsubsection{Research Focus}
The paper proposes an ensemble deep reinforcement learning (DRL) strategy for automated stock trading.  
The aim is to combine the strengths of multiple DRL algorithms to create a more robust and adaptive trading agent capable of handling dynamic market regimes.

\subsubsection{Methodology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Algorithms used}: PPO, A2C, and DDPG (all actor–critic based).  
    \item \textbf{Load-on-demand technique}: Efficient data handling to reduce memory overhead in continuous action spaces.  
    \item \textbf{Ensemble mechanism}:  
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Agents are trained on rolling windows of historical data.  
        \item A validation window selects the best agent based on Sharpe ratio and turbulence index.  
        \item The selected agent executes trades in the next trading period.  
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dataset}: 30 Dow Jones Industrial Average (DJIA) stocks.  
    \item \textbf{Baselines}: Compared against DJIA index and minimum-variance portfolio.  
\end{itemize}

\centering

\begin{tabular}{|p{4cm}|p{8.5cm}|}

\textbf{Component} & \textbf{Description} \\
\hline
Algorithms & PPO, A2C, DDPG (actor–critic DRL) \\
\hline
Selection metric & Sharpe ratio (risk-adjusted), turbulence index (risk control) \\
\hline

    \item Achieves higher Sharpe ratios and more stable risk-adjusted returns.  
    \item Demonstrates adaptability by selecting agents that perform best in different market regimes.  
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Computationally expensive due to multiple agents and retraining.  
    \item Performance depends heavily on window size, hyperparameters, and market conditions.  
    \item Limited to three DRL algorithms — future work could expand to newer methods (e.g., SAC, TD3).  
    \item Still evaluated only in backtesting; real-world trading deployment remains untested.  
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Research Focus}
The paper addresses the difficulty of applying DRL to financial time series, which suffer from low signal-to-noise ratios and irregular patterns.  
It proposes a cascaded LSTM architecture to better capture temporal dependencies and latent structures in daily stock data.

\subsubsection{Methodology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Two-stage cascaded LSTM:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Feature extraction stage:} First LSTM learns temporal patterns from raw daily stock data.  
        \item \textbf{Agent stage:} Extracted features are fed into a DRL agent (PPO) enhanced with another LSTM in its actor–critic networks.  
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Datasets:} Evaluated on four major indices — DJI (US), SSE50 (China), SENSEX (India), FTSE100 (UK).  
    \item \textbf{Benchmarks:} Compared against buy-and-hold, PPO-MLP, standalone LSTM/MLP, LightGBM, gradient boosting, and ensemble DRL strategies.  
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Results}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Achieved performance improvements of \textbf{5–52\%} across cumulative returns, max earning rate, and average profit per trade.  
    \item Outperformed both traditional ML baselines and DRL models without cascaded LSTM.  
    \item Showed particularly strong results in the SSE50 market, highlighting adaptability in emerging markets.  
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Increased complexity and training cost due to two-stage architecture.  
    \item Sensitivity to hyperparameters, market regimes, and data frequency.  
    \item Results based on backtesting only — no live trading validation.  
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Commission Fee is not Enough: A Hierarchical Reinforced Framework for Portfolio Management \cite{xu2022commission}}

\subsubsection{Research Focus}
This work highlights a key limitation in many DRL-based portfolio management systems: most models only consider commission fees as trading costs, neglecting the impact of \textbf{price slippage}, which can significantly alter execution outcomes.  
To address this, the authors propose \textbf{HRPM}, a hierarchical framework that separates portfolio allocation from trade execution.

\subsubsection{Methodology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Two-tier hierarchical design:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{High-level policy:} Allocates portfolio weights at lower frequency, aiming to maximize long-term returns.  
        \item \textbf{Low-level policy:} Executes trades within short windows, minimizing costs including slippage.  
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Training strategy:}  
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Pre-training both levels to establish baseline behaviors.  
        \item Iterative joint training for coordinated strategy–execution efficiency.  
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Evaluation:} Conducted on both U.S. and Chinese stock markets against DRL and traditional baselines.  
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Results}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Trading costs:} Commission fees are only a minor part of total costs — slippage dominates, especially in large trades.  
    \item \textbf{Entropy bonus:} Adding entropy to rewards diversifies portfolios.  
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $\eta = 0.05$ → highest annualized return (ARR).  
        \item $\eta = 0.1$ → lowest maximum drawdown (MDD).  
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Execution behavior:} The low-level policy spreads trades strategically — selling partially early and timing buys/sells with local price trends to reduce slippage.  
    \item \textbf{Overall performance:} HRPM outperforms state-of-the-art DRL and classical portfolio strategies in both U.S. and Chinese markets.  
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Increased complexity from two-tier architecture requires higher training costs.  
    \item Sensitivity to hyperparameters (e.g., entropy weight $\eta$) can shift balance between risk and return.  
    \item Tested only in backtesting — lacks real-time trading validation.  
    \item Execution dynamics may vary under different market conditions not covered in the study.  
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Methodology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Data fusion pipeline:}
    \begin{itemize}

        \item \textbf{Fusion:} Combined features from both branches form the state representation.  
    \end{itemize}

\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Results}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Outperformed traditional baselines and single-modality DRL models.  
    \item Demonstrated higher profitability and improved Sharpe ratios, reflecting stronger risk-adjusted returns.  
    \item Effective across both Chinese stock datasets and selected S\&P 500 stocks, suggesting cross-market robustness.  
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Computationally more complex due to dual-branch feature extraction and fusion.  
    \item Performance may depend heavily on candlestick chart quality and preprocessing choices.  
    \item Still limited to historical backtesting — no live trading validation.  
    \item Fusion limited to three modalities; other information sources (e.g., sentiment/news data) were not considered.  
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Methodology}

    \item \textbf{Dual-agent design:}
    \begin{itemize}

        \item \textbf{Agent 2:} Uses a \textbf{Multi-Scale CNN} to capture temporal features at different resolutions.  
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Algorithmic backbone:} Both agents are trained with \textbf{Double DQN (DDQN)}, reducing Q-value overestimation bias.  
    \item \textbf{Pre-training and fine-tuning:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Pre-trained on a mixed dataset of major U.S. indices for generalization.  
        \item Fine-tuned on specific assets for scenario adaptation.  
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Results}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Achieved an average cumulative return of \textbf{23.08\%} across five stock indices.  
    \item Outperformed single-agent models and other baselines in terms of profitability and robustness.  
    \item Demonstrated strong \textbf{generalization}: pre-training enabled transferability, while fine-tuning stabilized performance across assets.  
    \item Multi-agent design produced more stable risk-return profiles than standalone DRL systems.  
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Computational overhead due to maintaining and coordinating multiple agents.  
    \item Dependence on feature extractors (TimesNet, CNN) may limit adaptability in radically different market regimes.  
    \item Tested only on historical backtests — no live trading experiments.  
    \item Focused primarily on U.S. stock indices; cross-market scalability remains to be validated.

\section{Hybrid Approaches: Integrating LLMs with RL in Finance}

\subsection{FINCON: A Synthesized LLM Multi-Agent System with Conceptual Verbal Reinforcement for Enhanced Financial Decision Making \cite{xu2023fincon}}

\subsubsection{Research Focus}
FINCON addresses the complexity of sequential financial decision-making by combining LLMs with reinforcement learning in a \textbf{multi-agent system}.  
It introduces a manager–analyst hierarchy, conceptual verbal reinforcement, and dual-level risk control to produce more robust, interpretable, and risk-aware financial strategies.

\subsubsection{Methodology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Multi-agent structure:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Market Analyst Agents:} Extract and summarize raw market data (e.g., stock prices, fundamentals).  
        \item \textbf{Sentiment \& News Agents:} Process financial news, reports, and social media, converting unstructured text into sentiment insights.  
        \item \textbf{Strategy Agents:} Propose candidate trading or portfolio actions by integrating analyst and sentiment outputs.  
        \item \textbf{Risk Management Agents:} Monitor exposure, apply risk constraints, and manage portfolio-level safety.  
        \item \textbf{Manager (Decision-Maker) Agent:} Synthesizes all inputs and finalizes portfolio or trading decisions.  
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Risk control:}  
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Within-episode → monitors CVaR daily to limit short-term risk.  
        \item Over-episode → updates beliefs and strategies after each trading cycle.  
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Conceptual Verbal Reinforcement (CVRF):} Agents self-critique and share distilled “conceptual beliefs” in natural language to guide future decisions.  
    \item \textbf{POMDP formulation:} Frames trading/portfolio tasks as POMDPs with latent factors (e.g., macro uncertainty).  
    \item \textbf{Prompt optimization:} Uses text-based gradient descent to refine prompts across episodes for consistent reasoning.  
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Results}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Outperforms baseline DRL agents and other LLM-based approaches.  
    \item Improves \textbf{cumulative return (CR)}, \textbf{Sharpe ratio (SR)}, and reduces \textbf{maximum drawdown (MDD)}.  
    \item Manager–analyst hierarchy improves efficiency by reducing communication overhead.  
    \item CVRF enhances interpretability and generalization in both single-stock and portfolio tasks.  
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Computationally demanding due to multiple specialized agents and multimodal inputs.  
    \item Relies on accurate prompt engineering and high-quality datasets.  
    \item Current validation limited to backtests; no live trading deployment.  
    \item Scaling the agent network may introduce coordination complexity.  
\end{itemize}
